# CCA 106 — Lesson 3: Understanding Client Motivations & Decision Drivers

**Module:** CCA 106 — Risk Advisory Practice, Business Development & Client Growth
**Video duration:** ~45 minutes | **Workshop:** ~25 minutes | **Independent:** ~15 minutes

---

## 1. Lesson Overview

This lesson teaches why clients make the decisions they do. Learners discover that
motivation is rarely price alone — it can be safety, continuity, reputation, family, or
peace of mind — and that the same risk can mean different things to different clients.
The lesson provides discovery questions that reveal decision drivers and a professional
way to handle the price objection without arguing.

## 2. Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, learners will be able to:

1. List the common decision drivers that motivate clients.
2. Explain why the same risk produces different motivations in different clients.
3. Use discovery questions instead of "Do you want insurance?" prompts.
4. Invite affordability into the discussion without weakening the advice.
5. Respond to the price objection professionally, without defensiveness.

## 5. Learner Handbook

### 5.1 The motivator map

Motivations are rarely one-dimensional. Safety, financial security, continuity,
reputation, compliance, family, peace of mind, growth, convenience, protection of
assets, protection of income, and protection of dependants can all sit behind a single
decision. The advisor's job is to find which one governs *this* client.

### 5.2 Same risk, different driver

One school proprietor wants to keep parents' confidence; another cannot afford to
rebuild; a third must satisfy regulators. The risk is the same; the decision driver is
different — and so is the recommendation narrative. Assume nothing.

### 5.3 Discovery questions

Replace "Do you want insurance?" with questions that reveal what matters:
- "What concerns you most about this exposure?"
- "What would be the biggest consequence if this happened?"
- "What would you most want to protect?"
- "What would make you comfortable with the proposed approach?"

### 5.4 Affordability as information

When a client says cash flow is the concern, welcome it into the discussion. The
advisor's discipline is to separate what needs immediate attention from what can be
implemented progressively — affordability shapes sequencing, not whether advice is given.

### 5.5 The price conversation

Never argue that "the policy is worth it." Acknowledge cost as important, anchor back to
the risks that matter most to the client, then examine scope and options. Price is a
conversation about value, not a contest to win.

## 6. Practical Exercises

**Exercise 3.1 — Motivation interview (15 min)**
Interview a partner about a recent purchase decision. Use the four discovery questions.
Then write down the driver they revealed and what it would change about how you would
advise them.

**Exercise 3.2 — Three clients, one risk (20 min)**
Take the SR-001 exposure (property protection). Write a different opening for Client A
(reputation), Client B (can't rebuild), and Client C (compliance). Compare how the
language changes.

**Exercise 3.3 — Price objection role-play (15 min)**
Role-play the "premium is too expensive" objection in partners. One partner must use the
BETTER response pattern three times against increasingly aggressive objections, never
arguing and never switching to fear.

## 7. Case Study

**Scenario:** Florence James wants to protect Flourish College "because I cannot afford
to rebuild and the parents trust this school." Her CoverScore is 18 — Critical Risk —
and she has already mentioned cash-flow pressure. She asks, "Your premium is too
expensive, and honestly the school can't pay much."

**Tasks:**
1. Identify her primary decision driver from the statement.
2. Write her motivation-based opening for a conversation about Property Protection.
3. Draft the BETTER price response, sequenced so that immediate attention items come
   before progressive implementation.
4. Explain how cost becomes part of the discussion rather than a blocker.

## 8. Knowledge Check

1. Which statement is true about client motivation?
   a. All clients are motivated by price. *(incorrect)*
   b. The same risk can have different decision drivers in different clients. *(correct)*
   c. Motivation is only about saving money. *(incorrect)*
   d. Motivation cannot be discovered. *(incorrect)*

2. Which question best replaces "Do you want insurance?"
   a. "Let me give you the cheapest option." *(incorrect)*
   b. "What would be the biggest consequence if this happened?" *(correct)*
   c. "Everyone in your position buys this." *(incorrect)*
   d. "How much can you afford?" *(incorrect)*

3. Client says, "My biggest concern is cash flow." The professional response:
   a. "You shouldn't let that stop you." *(incorrect)*
   b. "That's important — let's make affordability part of the discussion so we separate what needs immediate attention from what can be implemented progressively." *(correct)*
   c. "Cash flow matters less than your risk." *(incorrect)*
   d. "We have payment plans for exactly this." *(incorrect)*

4. Client says, "Your premium is too expensive." The professional response:
   a. "But the policy is worth it." *(incorrect)*
   b. "You're wrong — this is the best value." *(incorrect)*
   c. "I understand cost is important. Before we discuss price alone, let's ensure the protection addresses the risks that matter most, then look at scope and options." *(correct)*
   d. "Fine, I'll lower the price." *(incorrect)*

5. "Protection of dependants" is an example of:
   a. A pricing strategy *(incorrect)*
   b. A decision driver *(correct)*
   c. A policy exclusion *(incorrect)*
   d. A compliance rule *(incorrect)*

**Answers:** 1-b, 2-b, 3-b, 4-c, 5-b.

## 9. AI Tutor Knowledge Base

- **KB-3.1 Decision drivers:** Safety, financial security, continuity, reputation,
  compliance, family, peace of mind, growth, convenience, protection of assets/income/
  dependants. Discover, never assume.
- **KB-3.2 Same risk, different driver:** The same exposure can be framed around
  reputation, rebuild cost, or regulation. The recommendation narrative changes with the
  driver.
- **KB-3.3 Discovery questions:** Concern, consequence, protection, comfort — the four
  questions that replace the "Do you want insurance?" prompt.
- **KB-3.4 Affordability:** Invite cost into the discussion. Separate immediate-attention
  actions from progressive implementation.
- **KB-3.5 Price objection pattern:** Acknowledge cost, anchor to the client's priority
  risks, then examine scope and options. Never argue ("worth it") and never fear-sell.

## 10. AI Coaching Prompts

- **Prompt 3.1 (Driver discovery):** "Play three different clients facing the same
  exposure with three different drivers (reputation, rebuild cost, compliance). The
  learner must discover the driver using discovery questions within four turns."
- **Prompt 3.2 (Price objection):** "Push the learner with escalating price objections.
  They must hold the professional pattern each round — acknowledge, anchor to priority
  risks, then scope and options — without arguing or fear-selling."
- **Prompt 3.3 (Motivation map):** "Give the learner a client's messy statement of
  concerns. Ask them to identify the likely primary driver, write the two discovery
  questions they would ask to confirm it, and say how the recommendation would differ
  under an alternative driver."

## 11. Lesson Completion Standard
